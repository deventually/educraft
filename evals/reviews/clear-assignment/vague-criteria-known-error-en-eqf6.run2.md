### What are you going to do?

You write a literature review for Academic Skills 2. A literature review is a text that sums up and discusses what researchers have already written about a topic.

- You work individually.
- You choose one of the topics on the course page.
- Your review is 3000–3500 words long.

[to be filled in by the teacher: why this assignment? What does it add for the subject or the profession?]

### Steps

1. Choose one topic from the list on the course page.
2. Find your sources. You need at least 15 sources. At least 10 of them must be peer-reviewed journal articles. Peer-reviewed means that other experts checked the article before it was published.
3. Write your literature review on your own. It is 3000–3500 words long.
4. Use Harvard referencing throughout. Harvard referencing is a fixed way to show which sources you used.
5. Submit your review via Moodle by 30 May, 17:00.

## Open points

1. **Purpose:** You name no purpose or value for this assignment. Students then do not know why they write the review or how it helps them in their studies or profession.
2. **File type and form:** You name no file type or form (for example Word or PDF, title page, layout). Students then do not know what to upload in Moodle.
3. **Word count:** You do not say whether the 3000–3500 words include the reference list. Students may then write too much or too little.
4. **Weighting of criteria:** You do not say how the four criteria count towards the grade. Students then cannot see what matters most.
5. **Criterion "of good quality":** This is not observable. Students cannot tell what "good quality" means. Suggestion: describe what the lecturer sees in a review of good quality (for example in a short rubric). Only add this if it matches what you already assess.
6. **Criterion "sufficiently critical of the literature":** This is not observable. "Sufficiently" has no clear limit. Suggestion: describe what a critical review shows (for example comparing studies or naming limits of studies), if that is what you assess.
7. **Criterion "a professional attitude is shown":** This is not observable. It is unclear which behaviour or which part of the text shows it. Suggestion: name what you look at, or explain how a student can show this in a written review.
8. **Criterion "has clearly made an effort":** This is not observable. Students cannot tell how you see effort in a finished text. Suggestion: name the observable signs of effort, or check whether this criterion belongs in the assessment.
9. **Other sources:** You require 15 sources, of which 10 are peer-reviewed journal articles. You do not say which types the other 5 may be. Students may then doubt whether, for example, books or websites are allowed.
10. **Topic list:** You refer to "the course page" but give no name or link. Students may not find the list quickly.
11. **Questions:** You name no contact person or place for questions. Students then do not know whom to ask.
12. **Resit and intermediate moments:** You name no resit rules and no intermediate or feedback moments. Students who fail, or who want feedback before the deadline, do not know what is possible.
13. **Language of the review:** You do not say in which language students write the review.
14. **Technical terms:** The short explanations of "literature review", "peer-reviewed" and "Harvard referencing" are kept general. Please check that they match how you use these terms in the course.

## What I changed

- **Goal first:** The text now starts with what students do, in a fixed place. The purpose has only a marker, because the original names none.
- **Steps numbered:** I put the actions in the order the original gives them: choose a topic, find sources, write, reference, submit. I added no new steps, deadlines or feedback moments.
- **Requirements as a list:** Length, number of sources, the 10 peer-reviewed articles and Harvard referencing now have one visible place. In the original they sat together in one sentence.
- **Submission information together:** Moodle and "30 May, 17:00" are now under one heading, with a marker for questions.
- **Technical terms explained:** I explained "literature review", "peer-reviewed" and "Harvard referencing" in one short sentence each. The terms "peer-reviewed" and "Harvard referencing" stay word for word.
- **Sentences shortened and active:** For example, "The lecturer will look at whether the student is sufficiently critical" became "you are sufficiently critical of the literature".
