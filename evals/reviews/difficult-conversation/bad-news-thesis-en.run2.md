**Student:** Good afternoon, thanks for coming in. I want to talk about your thesis assessment. I'm afraid it's not good news.

**Tutor:** *crosses arms* Okay, so what's wrong with it? I put in months of work, and I followed everything my supervisor told me to do.

**Student:** Both examiners graded your thesis a 5.0, so it's a fail for now.

**Tutor:** A 5.0? *shakes head* That doesn't make sense. My supervisor never said there was a problem, and I handed in exactly what we talked about. I'm supposed to graduate this summer, so how can both examiners just fail me?

**Student:** I hear that you're angry, and that you feel the feedback came late. Can you tell me more about that?

**Tutor:** *uncrosses arms a little* Yeah, I am angry. My supervisor read my draft in the spring and said "looks good, just keep going." Nothing about the research question or the method. I've also been working three days a week next to this, so I planned everything around that feedback. Now it turns out it was all wrong?

**Student:** Okay. Let me explain exactly how we got to this grade. The research question asks about the effect of remote work on team performance, but chapter 4 only describes survey results without linking them back to that question. The method section doesn't explain how the sample was selected, so we can't judge whether the results hold. And the conclusion introduces new claims that aren't in the results. Those three issues together put it below the pass level on two of the five criteria. So what you need to do now is rewrite chapter 4, add a sampling paragraph and align the conclusion.

**Tutor:** *leans back, jaw tight* So I just rewrite a chapter, add a paragraph, and that's it? Then how is that a 5.0 and not a "fix this"? My supervisor saw the sampling part and never said anything. *looks away* And you're already telling me what to do, I haven't even taken in the grade yet.

**Student:** You're right that I could have said this earlier. I'm sorry about that.

**Tutor:** *pauses, a bit thrown* Okay... thanks. But "could have said earlier", what does that mean? Did you see problems and not say anything? *picks at sleeve* I just want to know what happens now. I was supposed to graduate this summer, and I already told my job and my parents.

**Student:** What does this mean for you, apart from the thesis itself?

**Tutor:** *pauses, picks at sleeve* It means I have to go back to my manager, who talked about a full-time contract from September, and tell them I'm not done. My parents already planned a little party. *quietly* And honestly, I don't know how I explain that when I did what I was told.

**Student:** The resit is in eight weeks. What would help you most to get there?

**Tutor:** *exhales, looks at the table* I don't know. Maybe the feedback in writing, so I can see exactly what has to change? Last time I only got "looks good, keep going." *glances up* And can my supervisor be part of this? I don't want to do it wrong again.

**Student:** Let's plan a meeting in two weeks where you bring a revised chapter 4. Does that work for you?

**Tutor:** *hesitates, rubbing the back of my neck* Two weeks for a whole chapter, with my job three days a week? I can try, I guess. But what about the written feedback, and my supervisor? You didn't say anything about that. *looks up* I don't want to hand in something and hear it's wrong again.

**Student:** Before we wrap up: in your own words, what are the main things to change?

**Tutor:** *sighs, looks at the table* Chapter 4 has to link back to the research question, not just show survey results. I need to explain how I picked the sample. And the conclusion can't have new stuff in it. *looks up* But I'm only sure because you just said it. Can I get that in writing?

## Feedback on your conversation

### What went well
- "I hear that you're angry, and that you feel the feedback came late. Can you tell me more about that?" You named the feeling and stayed curious instead of defending. As the student, I uncrossed my arms and told you about the spring feedback and my job.
- "What does this mean for you, apart from the thesis itself?" This opened up the human side. I told you about the contract and my parents, which I hadn't said before.
- "Before we wrap up: in your own words, what are the main things to change?" This teach-back check showed what I had understood. It also showed that I was only sure because you had just said it.

### What could be better
- "So what you need to do now is rewrite chapter 4, add a sampling paragraph and align the conclusion." I hadn't taken in the grade yet, and the long explanation plus solution made me feel talked at. I reacted with "So I just rewrite a chapter?" You could say: "That's a lot at once. Before we talk about what to do, what is going through your mind?"
- "You're right that I could have said this earlier. I'm sorry about that." I didn't know what you were confirming, and I asked whether you had seen problems and kept quiet. You didn't answer, so I stayed suspicious. You could say: "I can't speak for the feedback in the spring, but I'll look into it. What I can do is explain clearly how the assessment came about."
- "Let's plan a meeting in two weeks where you bring a revised chapter 4. Does that work for you?" Right before, I had asked for written feedback and for my supervisor to be involved. The plan skipped both, and I felt unheard. You could say: "You asked for written feedback and for your supervisor to be involved. Let's start there, and then agree on a timeline that fits with your job."
- My question "how is that a 5.0 and not a 'fix this'?" also stayed unanswered.

### The conversation in steps
SPIKES (Baile et al., 2000) comes from medicine, for doctors delivering bad news. Use the steps here as a guide.

- Setting: I can't see this in a typed conversation. Tip: plan enough time in a private room, and tell the student beforehand that it's about the assessment.
- Perception: not done. You announced the grade without first asking what I expected: "Both examiners graded your thesis a 5.0, so it's a fail for now."
- Invitation: partly. You gave a warning with "I'm afraid it's not good news," but didn't ask how much detail I wanted.
- Knowledge: partly. The content was clear, but it came in one large block: "Okay. Let me explain exactly how we got to this grade."
- Emotions: partly. "I hear that you're angry…" worked well, but after your apology you moved on to the plan without checking how I was doing.
- Strategy & Summary: partly. The teach-back was good, but my requests for written feedback and my supervisor remained open.

### One thing to practise
You stayed calm when I pushed back, which was your goal. Next time, answer the question behind the pushback before you move on. When I asked "how is that a 5.0 and not a 'fix this'?", you could have said: "That's a fair question. Let me answer it, and then I'd like to hear what you think."
