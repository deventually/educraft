## When to use this tool

Use this tool when you want to prepare **one lesson**: you know what the students should be able to do at the end, and you want a plan with a time plan you can teach tomorrow. You get a **draft**: activating prior knowledge, explanation in small steps, guided practice, a check for understanding, independent practice and a closing, with the actual questions and problems per phase. You know the group and adapt the lesson.

Want to build a whole lesson or course backward from learning outcomes and assessment? Use **Design a lesson or course** (Backward Design) instead.

## How it works

1. **Enter the learning goal**, with an observable verb (calculates, explains, writes). With "understands" or "knows", the tool proposes a sharper wording and builds the lesson on it.
2. **Describe the content** and, if you know it, what the students already know.
3. **Enter the lesson length, the group size and the delivery mode.** The activities adapt to them: in a lecture or an online lesson, all students answer at once.
4. **Name fixed parts**, such as announcements or a break. They get their own place in the time plan.

## What the tool does and doesn't do

- **Does:** a time plan that adds up to your lesson length, every phase linked to your learning goal, a decision point at the check for understanding (do about 80% get it right?), and questions to come back to the material next week and next month (Rosenshine, 2012).
- **Does:** say honestly when the content does not fit the time, or when prior knowledge is missing.
- **Doesn't:** replace your learning goal, invent sources or add material you did not name.

## Tips

- **Check the worked examples and subject content** before you use them. The tool does its best, but you are the subject expert.
- **Too much content?** Take the proposal and move the rest to a later lesson. Small steps with practice work better than everything at once.

> Original LimeOnIt instrument. Basis: Rosenshine, B. (2012), *Principles of Instruction: Research-Based Strategies That All Teachers Should Know*, American Educator 36(1), 12–19, 39.
